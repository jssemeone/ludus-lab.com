AES: A Cipher With No Secrets?

AES is open and, after twenty years, unbroken. So why do Russia, China and other powers build their own ciphers instead of just adopting it?

⏱️ 6 min read
📊 Intermediate level
🔒 The standard behind HTTPS, Wi-Fi and disks

If AES is so open, why doesn’t everyone use it?

The full specification of AES is published and available to anyone: it’s written down in an open standard, built into every browser and phone, and explained in textbooks. People have been trying to break it in public for more than twenty years — without success. You’d think it would be the one shared standard for everyone. But no: Russia adopted its own cipher — Kuznyechik, China adopted SM4, and other countries have their own standards too.

If the algorithm is open and in twenty years no one has found a single practical flaw in it, what drives entire states to spend years building their own cipher instead of using the ready-made one? Maybe the "transparency" of AES isn’t so complete after all?

Key takeaway: Mathematically AES really is open — and that’s its strength. But a state’s trust in a cipher is about more than math. The question is less "is there a secret in the algorithm itself" and more "who wrote the standard and who controls it." That’s exactly where the line runs that gives rise to national ciphers.

How AES is built

First let’s see why there’s almost nothing to criticize in the math of AES — and then come back to why that doesn’t convince everyone. AES is a substitution-permutation network (SP-network): instead of splitting the block in half like a Feistel network, it keeps the block in a matrix of bytes and transforms the whole thing each round with four layers of operations. So every operation has to be reversible.

1

The block is a matrix

The data block is laid out across the cells of a square matrix. In real AES that’s a 4×4 matrix of 16 bytes (128 bits); in the Ludus Lab game it’s a 2×2 matrix of four nibbles (16 bits). Every later step works on the rows and columns of this matrix.

2

SubBytes — the S-box substitution

Each byte is replaced by another according to a fixed table — the S-box. This is "confusion": the single non-linear operation in AES. Without it, the whole cipher would collapse into a system of linear equations and could be solved directly.

3

ShiftRows and MixColumns — mixing

The rows of the matrix are cyclically shifted, and the columns are mixed together. This is "diffusion": within a couple of rounds, one changed input bit spreads across the whole block. Both operations are linear and reversible.

4

AddRoundKey — adding the key

The state is XORed with this round’s key. The round keys are derived in advance from the shared key by a separate procedure — the key schedule. This is exactly the step you can’t undo without knowing the key.

5

Repeating rounds and decryption

The layers repeat many times: 10, 12 or 14 rounds depending on the key length. The last round has no MixColumns — it would add nothing to the strength. To decrypt, Bob runs the inverse operations in reverse order — but only if he knows the key.

Every step is reversible — but together they entangle the block with the key so thoroughly that you can’t run it backwards without the key.

Why you can’t run it backwards: You can’t undo the key addition without knowing the key. And the non-linear S-box plus a dozen rounds mean that any attempt to "untangle" the cipher without the key runs into a monstrously complex system of equations. Confusion hides the relationship between key and ciphertext; diffusion smears each bit across the whole block — and repeating both layers leaves no shortcut other than trying every key.

Open — but whose?

AES was chosen with unprecedented openness: from 1997 to 2000 the US standards institute (NIST) ran an international competition — 15 ciphers from around the world and three years of public analysis, with anyone free to hunt for weaknesses. The winner was Rijndael, by the Belgians Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen, and in 2001 it became the standard. That’s an honest process and a genuine strength of AES. And yet the openness of the algorithm is not the same as trusting whoever chose it and whoever holds the standard in their hands.

The distrust has roots in history. The NSA once secretly altered the S-boxes of the DES cipher — it later turned out to be for defense against an attack, but twenty years of secrecy bred suspicion. And in 2013 the Dual_EC_DRBG affair surfaced — a NIST-standard random number generator into which the NSA had, it appears, built a backdoor. Nothing of the kind has been found in AES itself — and in fact its S-box is built from an open algebraic rule, not from "magic" numbers. But for a state, "probably clean" is not a strong enough basis on which to entrust a cipher with its secrets.

Hence cryptographic sovereignty: your own cipher, your own regulator, audits by your own experts, and independence from a foreign standard. This doesn’t necessarily mean AES has a backdoor — it means national security isn’t built on trust in a foreign agency.

AES (USA, NIST)

The de facto world standard of the civilian internet: it encrypts almost all web traffic, Wi-Fi and disks. The regulator is the American NIST.

Open, but under foreign control

Kuznyechik (Russia)

The Russian standard GOST R 34.12-2015 — also an SP-network like AES: a 128-bit block, a 256-bit key, 10 rounds. The regulator is the FSB (TC 26).

Own cipher, own regulator

SM4 (China)

China’s national cipher (standard GB/T 32907): a 128-bit block and key, 32 rounds. It’s mandatory for many uses inside the country, such as Chinese Wi-Fi (WAPI).

China’s cryptographic sovereignty

And that’s not all: almost every major power has its own block cipher, and what they share is the SP-network scheme itself, on which most modern algorithms are built:

And what about Russia? Russia has two modern ciphers. "Kuznyechik" is an SP-network, a direct relative of AES. "Magma" (the former GOST 28147-89), on the other hand, is built differently — it’s a Feistel network. Both are fixed by current GOST standards; their modes of operation are defined in GOST R 34.13-2015.

The game’s main lesson: In AES every step is reversible, and mixing the columns is even linear — and yet without the key the cipher stays a blank wall. Its strength is assembled from openness, repetition, a single non-linear S-box, and the key. Why AES still hasn’t been broken

After twenty years of public analysis, the best attacks on AES-128 (such as "biclique") cut the search only to about 2126 instead of 2128 — barely any gain at all. In practice that means there’s no shortcut: all that’s left is trying every key, and that’s impossible.

Even a quantum computer doesn’t save the attacker: Grover’s algorithm only "halves" the strength, turning a 256-bit key into the equivalent of a 128-bit one. That’s why AES-256 is considered resistant even to the quantum threat — and why it’s recommended for long-term protection.

Where you use AES every day

HTTPS and the internet

The padlock in the address bar is most often AES-GCM: it encrypts almost all web traffic in TLS

Wi-Fi and messengers

WPA2/WPA3 protect your network with AES-CCM; encryption in popular messengers is built on AES too

Disks and phones

BitLocker, FileVault and smartphone storage encryption all use AES; processors speed it up with AES-NI instructions

Interesting facts

  • The name "Rijndael" is made from the authors’ surnames — Rijmen and Daemen; it’s pronounced roughly like "rain-dahl"
  • AES’s last round has no MixColumns: it’s linear and key-independent, so it could simply be stripped off the ciphertext — it adds no strength there
  • The S-box is the only non-linear part of AES, and its values aren’t "magic": they come from an open rule (the multiplicative inverse in the field GF(2⁸) plus an affine transformation). That’s exactly why the transparency of AES itself is hard to doubt.
  • The Rcon constants in the key schedule differ for each round — so the rounds don’t become identical and open the door to "slide attacks"
  • "Magma" (GOST) is a Feistel network, while "Kuznyechik" is an SP-network; curiously, Kuznyechik’s round keys are produced by a Feistel network
An unexpected conclusion: Being open and unbroken is a necessary condition, but not a sufficient one. A state entrusting a cipher with its secrets won’t rely on a standard written and controlled by another power. Trust in cryptography is not only mathematics but also power. That’s why Kuznyechik, SM4 and others appeared: the real question isn’t "is there a secret in AES" but "whose hand holds the pen that writes the standard."

Now you understand both how AES works and why its openness doesn’t make it the single cipher for everyone: where the math ends, the question of trust and control begins
